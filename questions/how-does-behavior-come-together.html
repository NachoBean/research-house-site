A broad question

How does behavior come together?

When someone reaches, remembers, hesitates, coordinates, or changes course, what makes that action happen? There is no single agreed-upon answer. Different perspectives direct our attention toward different parts of the process.

Begin before theory

First, describe what happened.

A useful inquiry separates the behavior we can observe from the explanation we prefer. The same pattern can often support several accounts, especially when those accounts make overlapping predictions.

At the crossroads

Compare consequences, not labels.

Perspectives become scientifically useful when we ask what each one predicts, where their predictions overlap, and what observation could make one account less plausible.

  • What does each perspective treat as the important unit of analysis?
  • Which variables does it expect to change behavior?
  • Can the same experiment support more than one explanation?
  • What evidence would genuinely distinguish the accounts?

From broad wonder to testable problem

A concrete case: why repeat an action that no longer works?

Infants’ repeated reaches in the A-not-B task provide one way to make the broad question experimentally manageable. The phenomenon is a case study; it is not the house’s front door.
