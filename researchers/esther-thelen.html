How does behavior come together? · Dynamic systems

Esther Thelen

A way of seeing behavior as something assembled in real time from the body, task, environment, and history—not merely executed from a fixed internal program.

One perspective among several This profile develops a dynamic-systems answer. Return to the broad question to compare it with cognitive-control, learning-history, and ecological or embodied approaches as those pathways grow.

01 · Foundation

How does organized behavior develop?

Begin by treating development as change in an active system. Perception, action, posture, attention, and prior experience cooperate to make a behavior possible in a particular moment.

Key shiftAsk what produces the behavior here and now before assuming a fixed ability or missing concept.

02 · Developing

Why do infants sometimes repeat an action that no longer succeeds?

The A-not-B task makes this question concrete. After repeatedly retrieving an object at location A, an infant may reach back to A even after watching it move to B. Thelen’s program asks how previous reaches, delays, visible cues, gaze, and posture contribute to that error.

03 · Advanced

How can a stable tendency emerge without a stored rule?

A dynamic account describes behavior as competition among influences that change over time. Repetition can strengthen one action tendency; current input can reinforce or overcome it; a change in bodily state can reorganize the field of possibilities.

Source

Thelen and colleagues argue that reaching reflects interacting perceptual, motor, postural, attentional, and historical processes.

Open

These findings do not by themselves prove that representation, working memory, or inhibition are unnecessary.

04 · Experiment record

Knowing in the Context of Acting

Smith, Thelen, Titzer, & McLin (1999) · Psychological Review, 106(2), 235–260

Six experiments altered reaching history, hidden-object presence, cue timing, attention, and posture. Together they show that A-not-B-like reaching is highly sensitive to the conditions under which the action unfolds.

Study scope
6 experiments; 177 experiment-participations reported across studies
Typical age
8- to 10-month-old infants
Primary outcome
First lid manually contacted
Strongest inference
Recent action history and current bodily/task conditions causally alter reaching

Read the evidence carefully

  • Condition sizes were commonly ten infants.
  • Repeated responses were generally not modeled as dependent.
  • Several manipulations combined multiple cues, limiting component-specific conclusions.
  • Confidence intervals and standardized effect sizes were not reported by the authors.
