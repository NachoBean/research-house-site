How does variation reveal organization? · Sensorimotor adaptation

Mark Shelhamer

A way of treating small fluctuations in performance as possible traces of how a sensorimotor system remembers errors, maintains stability, and prepares to adapt.

One contribution at the tableShelhamer's work joins nonlinear time-series reasoning to practical questions about vestibular function, rehabilitation, astronaut performance, and resilience. A temporal pattern may be informative without necessarily being fractal.

01 · Foundation

Why do people adapt differently?

Two people can begin with similar average performance yet respond differently to the same perturbation. Shelhamer's program asks whether the organization of their baseline behavior—not only its mean or spread—can help forecast that difference.

Key shiftPreserve the order of repeated observations. The sequence may carry information that disappears when trials are averaged.

02 · Developing

Can ordinary variability reveal adaptive capacity?

In vestibulo-ocular reflex work, performance fluctuates from one head movement to the next. Spectral slope can summarize the relative contribution of slower and faster changes in that sequence. This is different from asking only how variable the scores are.

03 · Advanced

Does the useful time scale of memory depend on the system?

In the 2017 VOR study, weaker longer-term inter-trial correlation accompanied greater adaptation, while earlier saccade work reported the opposite direction. The authors interpret this contrast through the different functional demands of the two eye-movement systems.

Source

The authors argue that baseline temporal structure can forecast later adaptation within a particular sensorimotor task.

Open

The study did not establish a person-wide “sensorimotor phenotype,” a fractal VOR process, or prediction across different motor systems.

04 · Experiment record

Strength of baseline inter-trial correlations forecasts adaptive capacity in the vestibulo-ocular reflex

Beaton, Wong, Lowen, & Shelhamer (2017) · PLOS ONE, 12(4), e0174977

Participants first produced roughly 420 rapid yaw head steps during a seven-minute baseline, then completed four five-minute blocks of VOR-gain adaptation while wearing ×0.5 lenses. The analysis asked whether the temporal organization of baseline gain predicted the change after adaptation.

Sample
12 healthy adults recruited; 2 excluded for excessive blinking; final n = 10
Design
Within-person baseline followed by 20 minutes of visual-vestibular adaptation
Measures
VOR gain, spectral slope β, and pre-to-post adaptation extent Δ
Main result
β and Δ: r² = .72, p < .01 after rank-ordered surrogate analysis

What the evidence supports

  • The ordering of baseline trials predicted later adaptation better than mean, standard deviation, skewness, or kurtosis.
  • Time-shuffling removed the relationship, supporting the importance of temporal order.
  • The authors found no convincing evidence that the baseline VOR series itself was fractal.

Read the evidence carefully

  • The final sample was ten people, making the association vulnerable to instability and influential cases.
  • Baseline and adaptation gain were measured with different movement forms and calculation procedures.
  • The design supports prediction and temporal-structure sensitivity, not the proposed neural-storage mechanism by itself.
  • The reported model was not validated in an independent sample or clinical population.
